## Slide 2
Generative AI in enterprise decision‑making refers to models that produce text, summaries, recommendations, or scenarios that help humans decide. In practice, that means a model that drafts options, ranks risks, or synthesizes evidence so a human can make a final call. The key is human‑in‑the‑loop accountability: the model provides output, but humans are responsible for evaluating it, validating it, and making the actual decision. In enterprise settings, GenAI outputs can feed decision pipelines by summarizing regulatory requirements, recommending actions based on risk scores, or generating scenario analyses for strategic planning. These outputs are valuable because they compress complexity and speed up decisions, but they also amplify governance expectations because they influence outcomes that impact customers, employees, and business continuity. The scope here is enterprise decision support, not autonomous decision‑making, which keeps accountability with decision owners.

It is also useful to frame decision‑making as a pipeline: data is gathered, GenAI produces candidate outputs, and a decision owner applies judgment, policy, and compliance checks. This means systems must label outputs as advisory, track who approved actions, and record the evidence used. Without these guardrails, GenAI output can be treated as authoritative even when it is uncertain. Defining scope early reduces ambiguity and aligns the system to governance requirements.

In other words, GenAI should be treated as a decision accelerator, not a decision maker. The moment the model output becomes the final answer, the organization loses the ability to challenge outcomes and show accountability, which is exactly what regulators and stakeholders expect.

These foundations set the stage for risk mitigation later in the pipeline and determine whether the system can pass governance audits.

## Slide 5
Ethical risks rise quickly when GenAI influences decisions. Training data can embed bias that skews recommendations, creating unequal outcomes for individuals or groups (Bender et al., 2021). The reasoning behind outputs can be opaque, so stakeholders may not understand why a recommendation appears, which makes accountability harder (Arrieta et al., 2020). Organizations also risk overreliance, where staff defer to the model rather than critically evaluating its output. This is especially risky when the model’s output is framed as a recommendation rather than a possibility. To mitigate this, auditing and accountability frameworks are required, including internal process controls and documentation that make decisions reviewable over time (Raji et al., 2020). The ethical goal is not only to avoid harm but to make decisions contestable and transparent.

Ethical risk management also requires stakeholder awareness: affected groups should understand how GenAI influences decisions and how to challenge outcomes. For enterprises, this means defining appeal processes, documenting data provenance, and tracking performance across different user groups. Ethical governance aligns with business risk because biased or opaque decisions can trigger legal, reputational, or operational harm.

## Slide 6
Security risks include data leakage, model extraction, and adversarial manipulation. Prompt injection can cause sensitive data to surface in outputs, and extraction attacks can recover training data from the model itself (Carlini et al., 2021). Membership inference attacks can reveal whether a specific record was in the training set, which is a privacy risk for enterprise data (Shokri et al., 2017). Adversarial examples and manipulation can distort outputs to mislead decision‑makers (Goodfellow et al., 2015). These risks mean GenAI must be secured as a critical system: access control, monitoring, and threat modeling are required before integrating outputs into decision pipelines. Enterprises should treat GenAI as a high‑value target and integrate security checks into the model lifecycle, not just at deployment.

Security controls should include red‑teaming, safe prompt handling, role‑based access, and data‑loss prevention. Teams should monitor for unusual query patterns and implement rate‑limits. For high‑impact decisions, outputs should be verified with secondary checks or deterministic rules. A defense‑in‑depth approach is necessary because GenAI systems combine software, data, and human workflows, which creates multiple points of vulnerability.
